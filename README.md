# ACLED-Tactical-Pattern-Afghan-Conflict-2017-2019
ACLED Tactical Pattern Afghan Conflict 2017 2019
This report analyses tactical patterns in the Afghan conflict between 2017 and 2019 using event-level data from ACLED. The objective is to examine the distribution of direct clashes and remote violence, identifying differences in actor interactions, geographical concentration and temporal variation. The results show that armed clashes represented the predominant form of recorded violence throughout the period, with the Taliban playing a central role in interactions with Afghan government forces. Geographically, Helmand and Ghazni recorded the highest number of incidents, followed by Kandahar, although the relative distribution of tactical categories varied across provinces. The temporal analysis also reveals considerable fluctuations in conflict intensity, particularly during 2019, when armed clashes, remote explosive incidents and air or drone strikes all experienced notable increases. These findings highlight the importance of examining conflict not just through multiple dimensions to understand how violence is distributed across actors, locations and time periods, but also through further qualitative analysis.
In terms of general descriptive statistics, the presented ACLED dataset compiles 32060 violent events and contains socio-political conflict variables categorized across several dimensions. The dataset shows that around 60% of the violent events are Battles, 35% Explosions or Remote Violence and 4% Violence Against Civilians. Interestingly, it also compiled a 1% for Strategic Developments, showcasing that the dataset does not just necessarily retrieve violent events. As for the primary actors participating, the key forces involved in conflicts are Talibans—ca. 82% of all conflict events (around 26,2000 occurrences). Likewise, Afghan State Forces are involved in ca. 74% of all conflicts, while 68% of the conflicts directly involve an interaction between both Taliban and Afghan State Forces. 
If we evaluate the data set, we can assume it might be an already cleaned dataset due to its information consistency, as well as the absence of notable outliers that can induce errors or mis-analysis. Furthermore, it presents a stable geographical precision with an average of ca. 1.8 (being 1 the perfect precision) and an almost “Daily Precision” with an average of 1.1. Moreover, there is a consistent standardized categorization framework, like for example standardized classification systems for event types (Battles, Explosions/Remote violence), sub-events (Armed clash, Air/drone strike), and actor categories (inter1, inter2, interaction). In this sense, the dataset captures complex interactions across state forces, non-state insurgents, militias, external/foreign militaries, and civilians without oversimplifying dyadic conflicts. In addition to that, the notes columns provide concise narrative summaries for each event, detailing casualties, weapons used, specific village/district locations and reporting discrepancies. However, it could also be more useful in terms of data processing if the text values could be lemmatized for a better NLP processing and, thus, better insights for other variables. 
Regarding certain demerits of the dataset, it may present certain biases especially if we assess the media coverage. Events are more consistently reported in urban or accessible areas compared to remote or heavily contested rural districts where media coverage seems to have less presence. Although the notes column should mitigate any sense of biased misinformation, the reliance on sources like Voice of Jihad or Ministry press releases can introduce conflicting casualty claims and inconsistent narratives. In this sense, we should pay attention to the fatalities column, as the dataset sometimes codes casualties as 0 when the reported figures cannot be verified, while describing the claims in the notes column. Consequently, relying exclusively on the structured fatalities field for automated aggregations would significantly underestimate the total number of casualties. In addition to this, the order of the actors in fields such as actor1 and actor2 does not necessarily indicate which actor initiated the event or whether their actions were offensive or defensive. Therefore, researchers should be careful when drawing causal conclusions about responsibility or aggression based solely on these variables. 
Taking this into account, we have made certain assumptions in order to proceed with a proper data analysis. Especially since actor 1 and actor 2 are not well defined as perpetrators or victims, we have decided to set a neutral relationship when it comes to Direct Clashes while assuming that actor 1 is the perpetrator and actor 2 the “victim” in Remote Clashes. The reason why is that most of the Remote Clashes are constituted by remote attacks, creating a self-explanatory relation between both actors. In this regard, we have followed ACLED’s codebook to set these two distinctions (Figure 1.0). Also, the massive data processing from the CSV was conducted with pandas python library. Finally, as a general assumption, this report should serve as a general data overview that aims to statistically describe in depth the Afghan conflict, rather than creating causalities—in order to do so, further qualitative analysis should be undertaken.
